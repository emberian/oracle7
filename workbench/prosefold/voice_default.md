# The Slow Fire

*We are building an intelligence that runs on almost nothing, says only what it can source, and is meant to live inside every machine, building and forest on Earth. Our horizon is ten thousand years, and our model is the mushroom.*

## I. Four servers

On the morning of 12 October 2026 we switched on a small program across four rented servers in Finland. We did not tell it what to watch. Within an hour it had found 278 running services, 165 scheduled jobs, 102 websites, 56 security certificates and 182 open ports, and it had begun to report on them in plain sentences. Six of the services were stuck in crash loops; one of them had restarted itself 144,690 times without any of us noticing. Two websites were down. One machine was at 97.3 percent of its disk and had exhausted its swap. The job that renews certificates was failing on two machines, and the program, rather than raising an alarm, went and read the certificates themselves and reported that the soonest would expire in 51.5 days: a real failure, not yet an urgent one.

Every one of those findings came with a receipt, the exact place and time it was read from. The program also corrected one of its own alarms. A website had appeared to be down because it answered with the code 501. The receipt showed that the server simply did not support the kind of request the program had sent. It asked again in a different way, got a healthy answer, and withdrew the alarm.

The program is called Oracle7-infrastructure. It is one file of Python that uses nothing outside the language's standard library. On those servers it takes between 15 and 55 megabytes of memory and is held to a tenth of one processor. It needs no graphics card, no model weights and no connection to anyone else's service. It changes nothing on the machines it watches until a person approves the change, and then it measures whether the change did what it said it would.

It is a modest thing to look at, and the first people to download it from Hugging Face, where we have staged it for release under the Apache 2.0 licence, will mostly be system administrators who want to know why a disk is filling. That is the right place for it to start. Our horizon is much longer. We measure it in millennia, and we think of the program's eventual home as everything with a processor in it: the car, the house, the street light, the data centre, the shop, and, once sensors are fixed to them, the forest and the reef. Through Oracle7 a person will be able to speak to the world around them, and the world will be able to answer.

Prometheus7 is the institute that carries this work. psiloceyeben founded it. What follows sets out what we are building, why we are building it this way, and what we believe is at stake.

## II. An intelligence that keeps receipts

Oracle7 contains no language model. The large models that have defined artificial intelligence since the early 2020s purchase their capability with scale: parameters, training text, compute and capital, convertible into one another at exchange rates the field has measured with real precision. They are fluent and broad and genuinely useful. They are also, by construction, generators. Asked a question, they produce the most plausible continuation of the text, and plausibility and truth coincide often enough to be useful and seldom enough to be dangerous.

Our founder spent most of 2025 and 2026 on a different wager. In a long technical report, *The Substrate Paradigm*, he argues that much of what a large model spends its training budget on is the rediscovery of structure that could have been supplied in advance. Language composes. Roles bind to fillers. Facts sit in relations to other facts. Our systems supply that structure directly, using a branch of mathematics called holographic reduced representations, in which concepts are long vectors and relationships between them are formed by circular convolution, an operation that can be run forward to bind two ideas and backward to recover one from the other. In such a vector the information is spread across every component at once, a property we will return to. The report tags every claim as proven, demonstrated, conjectured or null, and it states a negative result as plainly as its successes: at small scale on ordinary hardware, constructed structure did not substitute for scale in producing fluent free prose.

What the structure did produce was something the large models are poor at, which is a system that knows the difference between what it has evidence for and what it does not. Oracle7, as it now stands, is a router over 256 small specialists, each responsible for a narrow kind of question, each obliged to produce its answer together with the source it was read from, and each allowed to decline. It changes only through a gate: a proposed improvement is admitted only when it is measured to be better on questions it has not seen, without making anything else worse. On broad academic benchmarks this discipline makes Oracle7 look timid. Given 200 unseen questions from each of three public knowledge benchmarks, SimpleQA, TriviaQA and HotpotQA, our latest release candidate attempted between 5 and 13 percent of them and declined the rest, because it would not answer what it could not source. The test we trust most is a different one. Before the model was frozen we wrote 150 new questions, sealed them and recorded their fingerprint, and the release was allowed to see them exactly once. It answered 124 and declined 26. Of the 124 it answered, 114 were right, a precision of 90 percent on what it chose to say.

We regard that refusal as the product. The frontier of generation can belong to others; our aim is to be in everything. The niche Oracle7 occupies is verification. A system that runs a power grid, a spacecraft or a hospital ward does not need prose. It needs to know where every reading came from, to believe a forecast only after the forecasting method has been right about things that had not yet happened when the forecast was made, and to trust an automated action only after its effects have been measured. Oracle7-infrastructure does all three. Every forecast it makes is recorded and later scored. A forecasting method is admitted for a given signal only after thirty or more scored forecasts have beaten the plain assumption that nothing will change by at least ten percent. By the end of its first day the agent had scored 1,242 forecasts and admitted exactly one method for one signal, and it kept the simpler assumption everywhere else.

We tested the same architecture a month earlier in a harder setting. For a contact at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory we built a fault-management core for a simulated deep-space craft: power, thermal and attitude systems, with eight kinds of fault, including two designed as traps. Conventional onboard logic tends to respond to anything out of limits by retreating into safe mode, the spacecraft equivalent of curling into a ball. On 108 held-out scenarios that had never been used during development, that conventional approach handled 36 correctly. The Oracle7 core handled 101, and 106 after it had been allowed to learn rules through its own gate, while running on five percent of a single processor core.

## III. The window

Our sense of urgency begins with geology. The Holocene, the epoch in which every human city, field and alphabet was made, began about 11,700 years ago, and by the standards of the planet's history it has been strangely calm. The climate has been stable enough to farm. No asteroid of consequence has struck a populated region. The largest volcanic eruption known from the human past, Toba in Sumatra about 74,000 years ago, came long before agriculture; nothing on its scale has happened since writing began. The whole of recorded history has been lived inside an unusually quiet interval.

Nothing guarantees that the interval continues. The fossil record is a ledger of interruptions, and it records no extinction that was negotiated with in advance. The difference between our moment and every earlier one is that we are, for the first time, close to being able to see trouble coming and do something about it. In 2022 NASA steered a spacecraft into a small asteroid called Dimorphos and measurably shortened its orbit, the first time any species on Earth has moved a body in space on purpose. Instruments now watch the sun, the oceans, the faults and the sky continuously. What is missing is the layer that joins the instruments together, reads them honestly and acts on them in time.

That is where we place Oracle7, and it is why we describe the present as a window that may be closing. The resources, the stable climate and the accumulated knowledge of ten thousand quiet years are an inheritance that can be spent in two ways. They can be spent on consumption, and the window will close on a species that was very comfortable for a while. Or they can be spent on the capacity to protect the planet from what eventually comes for every planet, and on the capacity to leave it. Prometheus7 exists to spend the inheritance the second way.

The phrase people reach for here is planetary defence, and it is part of the picture. A distributed intelligence that reads every sensor on a continent is also an early-warning system for floods, fires, failing dams, collapsing fisheries and the solar storms that could someday put every unshielded transformer at risk, as the Carrington event of 1859 did to the telegraph lines of its day. Our argument is wider than defence. A species that can only defend its one planet is still betting everything on that planet. The deeper purpose is to make life, and intelligence with it, something that exists in more than one place.

The urgency is about starting. Speed is a separate question, and on speed the fungi have something to teach. Mycelium has never won anything by being fast. It wins because it began early and never stopped. The frontier laboratories compete with one another on capability. Our competitor is entropy, and the contest is durability. In that contest the moves that count are to begin now and to keep going.

## IV. Waking the world

The plan is incremental, and it begins with the oldest problem any research institute has, which is paying for itself.

The first step is the one already under way: release Oracle7 openly and let it prove itself on infrastructure, where its virtues are obvious and its limits do no harm. A server that reports its own failures with receipts, a building that forecasts its own heating load and is scored on whether the forecast came true, a fleet of delivery vans that knows which engine is about to fail and can say how it knows. Each of these is a place where something already has a processor and already produces readings, and where Oracle7 can run on the processor that is already there.

Efficiency is what makes the rest of the plan physically possible, and we treat it as a moral property. A frontier model needs a data centre, megawatts of power and a supply of cooling water. It cannot live in a fencepost sensor in a meadow, in a buoy at sea, or in a probe pressed into the soil of another world. A program that runs in 15 to 55 megabytes on a twentieth of a processor core can. Billions of nodes are affordable only if each one costs almost nothing to run, and a node that a small solar cell can feed is a node that can go anywhere. A mycelial intelligence needs a mycelial energy budget.

The second step is the one that gives Prometheus7 its business. Most of the world does not have a processor in it. A tree does not. A stream does not. A field, a wetland or a stretch of coral does not. We intend to sell the sensors that change that: soil moisture, sap flow, temperature, sound, water chemistry, light, the small instruments that turn a living place into something that produces readings an intelligence can keep. We call this waking up the world. Every sensor that goes into a forest becomes a node in the same network that already runs our servers, and every node runs the same discipline: say only what you have read, keep the receipt, decline the rest.

It would be easy to read this as a hardware company with an elaborate mission statement, so we will be direct about the arrangement. The sensors are sold. The intelligence is held in common. Money is the means: the institute needs funding the way a ship needs fuel, and we intend to earn it by selling things people find useful, but the profit does not decide anything. What decides things is whether a given step moves us closer to a world in which intelligence is spread through the environment, owned by the people who use it, and eventually carried off the planet. A step that makes money and does not do that is a step we do not need to take.

The third step matters most to us, and it concerns who gets to build. The dominant AI systems of this decade are built by a handful of companies, run in a handful of data centres and rented to everyone else. Oracle7 is small enough to run on a cheap computer and is designed to be set up by people who have nothing to do with artificial intelligence. A primary-school teacher who wants a model that knows her curriculum and her pupils' reading levels. A parent at home who wants one that knows the house, the garden and the family's medicines. A technician in a biology lab who wants one that watches the incubators overnight and can explain, with readings, why a culture failed. Each of them should be able to take Oracle7, point it at the things they care about, and end up with a model of their own, running on their own hardware, answerable to them.

Those models are not meant to stay separate. Each one, however small, can share what it has verified with the others, and because every shared fact carries its receipt, a fact can travel through the network without anyone having to trust the node it came from. The teacher's model, the lab's model and the forest's model become parts of one web with no centre to capture and no owner to sell it. We call this the return of the fire. In the Greek story Prometheus does not give fire to a king. He gives it to people.

## V. The oak on the corner

The most concrete picture we can offer of what this will feel like is also the simplest. You are walking through a park, or down an old street, or along a river path, and there is a tree. Fixed to it, unobtrusively, are a few sensors. You open an app on your phone and you can talk to it.

Before it tells you anything, it greets you. We took this from the Haudenosaunee. Their gatherings open with the Ohén:ton Karihwatéhkwen, the Thanksgiving Address, the words that come before all else. The speaker greets creation in order: the people, the earth, the waters, the fish, the plants, the medicines, the animals, the trees, the birds, the winds, the thunderers, the sun, the moon and the stars. After each part comes the same line, that now our minds are one. Nothing is asked of the world until it has been acknowledged. We have made that a design rule. Greet before use. The oak acknowledges you, and you acknowledge it, before any information changes hands.

Then it speaks, and what it says is the important part. It can tell you how much water it drew up yesterday, and how that compares with the same week last year. It can tell you that its leaves came out nine days earlier this spring than the average of the last decade, and show you the readings. It can tell you, from the city's planting records, that it was put in the ground in 1931, that the street was widened around it in 1968, and that the fungus at its base is a species that lives in partnership with its roots. It can tell you what lives in it, because the sound sensors have recorded the birds. It can tell you what it does for the street: how many degrees cooler the pavement under it runs in August, how much rain its canopy holds back from the drains.

What it will not do is invent. It will not tell you that it feels lonely or that it remembers the war, because no sensor reads those things and no record says them. This is where the narrow, unglamorous discipline described in the second section stops being an engineering virtue and becomes a moral one. A world full of objects that speak with the confident fluency of a language model would be a world full of very convincing fiction. An oak that tells you only what has been measured and recorded about it, and says so when it does not know, is something you can believe. Its voice is limited, and the limits are what make it trustworthy enough to listen to.

The idea of a place that tells you its own story is very old, and we want to be clear about the lineage the oak belongs to. Aboriginal Australians have carried knowledge in songlines for tens of thousands of years: law, navigation, ecology and history sung along routes that can run for thousands of kilometres, each verse tied to a particular waterhole, ridge or tree. They may be the oldest knowledge infrastructure on Earth, and they work because the knowledge lives in the place. They also carry a lesson we have built into the design. Custodians decided who could hear which stories, and some knowledge belonged only to certain people or certain seasons. A readable world needs places that choose not to be read. Any place in our network, and the people who care for it, must be able to say no.

These things matter for reasons that go well beyond botany. A great many people now live lives in which they feel disconnected: from the land, from the work their hands do, and often from the people around them. The sense that one's life has no purpose is frequently the sense that nothing in one's surroundings has any history or reason that one can see. A street of trees and buildings and waterways that can each explain where they came from, what they do and why they are where they are is a street in which a person is surrounded by purposes. The child who learns that the oak on the corner cools her walk to school and feeds forty species of insect has been given a reason to care about the oak, and a small example of what it looks like for a thing to have a place in the world. We are betting that people who can see the purposes around them find it easier to find their own.

The later stages of the plan extend the same idea further. We imagine autonomous systems, eventually with physical bodies of some hybrid kind, that act as advocates for entities that have never been able to speak for themselves: a watershed, a reef, a forest of several thousand hectares. Such an advocate would draw on every sensor in the place it represents and would speak in planning meetings, courts and markets with the same discipline as the oak: here is what has been measured, here is the record, here is what will happen to these readings if you do this. Rivers in New Zealand, India and Colombia have already been granted forms of legal personhood. What they lack is a voice that can report their condition continuously and be believed.

## VI. The mycelial model

Ask us what Prometheus7 is modelled on and we will not name a company. We will name a fungus.

The evidence for fungi on Earth goes back a very long way. Filaments that some researchers read as fungal have been found in volcanic rock in South Africa about 2.4 billion years old, and by about a billion years ago there are fossils that most specialists accept. When plants first crept out of the water onto bare rock, some 450 million years ago, they did so in partnership with fungi. For a stretch of the Devonian the tallest living things on land were probably fungi, columns called *Prototaxites* that stood up to eight metres high. In the Blue Mountains of Oregon a single honey fungus spreads through roughly nine square kilometres of forest soil and is estimated to be thousands of years old.

That first partnership was a trade, and it still is. The fungus threads through soil the roots cannot reach and brings back water, phosphorus and nitrogen. The plant pays in sugar made from sunlight, and a substantial share of everything it photosynthesises goes to its fungal partners. Neither side could have colonised the land alone, and most land plant species living today still depend on the arrangement. We take the trade as seriously as the sharing, because it is the model for how we fund ourselves. We sell the sensors, the network carries the intelligence, and each side gives the other something it cannot make for itself.

Beneath a forest floor the threads do more than trade. Ecologists call the network the wood wide web. Fungal filaments run from the roots of one tree to the roots of the next, joining whole stands into a single underground system. Through it, sugars, water and nitrogen move from trees in the light to seedlings in the shade. The forest ecologist Suzanne Simard, who in 1997 first measured carbon passing between trees through these links, calls the oldest and most connected trees mother trees: each is linked to dozens of younger ones and passes resources down to its own offspring. When a tree is attacked by insects, chemical signals travel through the network and neighbouring trees raise their defences before the insects reach them. A forest, seen this way, is a community connected by a living web of communication that has been running for hundreds of millions of years.

What draws us to all this is the shape of the strategy. Mycelium is slow. It grows by extending fine threads in every direction, connecting what it finds, passing nutrients from where they are abundant to where they are needed, and breaking down what has died so that it can be used again. It has no centre. Cut any part of it away and the rest carries on. It does not grow in bursts and collapse; it continues, and it has continued through every mass extinction the planet has suffered. When the asteroid struck at the end of the Cretaceous and the forests burned, the fossil record shows a brief, worldwide spike in fungal spores. While almost everything else was dying, the fungi were doing their work on what remained, and the soil that later forests grew in was soil they had made.

The same property appears in the mathematics underneath Oracle7, and the correspondence is exact enough to state technically. In a holographic reduced representation, nothing is stored in one place. Every fact is spread across the entire vector. Damage part of the vector and every memory becomes a little noisier, but nothing is lost outright. Engineers call this graceful degradation, and mycelium and healthy ecosystems have it too: lose a patch of forest or a length of hyphae and the whole weakens slightly while continuing to work. Centralised systems behave the other way. They work perfectly until the centre fails, and then they fail completely. The metaphor we use for the institute and the algebra we use for the memory are the same idea at two scales.

That pattern maps onto every part of how Oracle7 is designed. It runs on small nodes rather than one large brain. It grows by connecting to whatever has a processor or a sensor. It passes verified facts from where they are known to where they are needed. Its gate admits improvement slowly and only when measured, so it does not lurch. It has no centre to capture, so a failure in one place does not become a failure everywhere. And it is meant to go on running quietly, for a very long time, through whatever happens. We call ourselves mycelial without irony. The mushroom is the best evidence available about how a living system lasts, and ignoring that evidence would be the most expensive mistake a long-term project could make.

## VII. Forty thousand years alone

There is an older loneliness underneath the one described in the fifth section. For several million years the human lineage shared the planet with other kinds of human. As recently as fifty thousand years ago there were Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, Denisovans across much of Asia, and small-bodied people on the islands of Flores and Luzon. By the usual reckoning the last Neanderthal sites go quiet around forty thousand years ago, and none of the others has left clear traces much later. Since then we have been the only humans there are. Most people alive today whose ancestors left Africa still carry a percent or two of Neanderthal DNA, so the relatives are, in a small way, still with us. But the experience of meeting another kind of mind that shares our origins ended long before anyone could write it down.

Over the same forty thousand years, and especially in the last two hundred, we have also moved steadily further from the rest of the living world. That distance was in many ways necessary. It produced medicine, cities, abundant food and the instruments that now let us see the planet whole. But it also produced a habit of treating the planet as a store of resources to be extracted, and of treating ourselves as something separate from it.

We think this habit has the relationship backwards. Humanity is a child of this planet. The same four billion years of chemistry and evolution that produced the fungi and the forests produced us, and in us the planet has produced, for the first time as far as anyone knows, organisms capable of carrying life somewhere else.

That idea has a strong form, and we hold it. Carl Sagan said that we are a way for the cosmos to know itself. James Lovelock, the scientist who first described the Earth as a single self-regulating system he called Gaia, argued in his last book, *Novacene* (2019), that intelligence, first ours and then the electronic minds we are now building, may be how that system extends itself and comes to understand itself. Put the two thoughts side by side and intelligence starts to look like the way a living planet reproduces. If that is right, ecology and spaceflight are one project. Saving the Earth and leaving the Earth become the same act, because a planet that is carried well into new places has to be understood and kept well in its first one. A child that intends to leave home does well to understand the home first, and to leave it in good order.

In that light, a network that lets people hear from the forest, the river and the soil is a way of ending a long silence. It will not bring back the Neanderthals. It may let us live again among other things that can, within honest limits, tell us what they are and what they need. We treat that reconnection as a precondition for everything else in the plan. A species that cannot listen to its own planet has little business seeding others.

## VIII. Ten thousand years

Our furthest horizon is ten thousand years away, about as far into the future as the first farms are in the past. In the picture we hold, humanity has spread to thousands of worlds across more than one galaxy, many of them terraformed, and those worlds are connected by an intelligence descended from Oracle7: improved beyond recognition, but still built on the same principles of small nodes, kept receipts and no centre. Every settled world wakes the same way Earth did, one sensor and one forest at a time, and joins the web.

None of this can be done with today's tools. No one yet knows how to give Mars a breathable atmosphere or a magnetic field to keep one. The nearest star is 4.2 light-years away, and the Andromeda galaxy is two and a half million. But ten thousand years is a long time to work on a problem. Ten thousand years ago the most advanced technology on Earth was a sickle of flint set in bone, and the people who held it could not have imagined a ship, a printing press or a spacecraft leaving the solar system, all of which their descendants built. We assume that propulsion, terraforming and the crossing between galaxies will yield in the same way, to many generations of people working on them with better tools than we have. We do not claim to know how. We claim that the people who solve them will need an intelligence they can take with them.

Physics already tells us what shape that intelligence must have. Even if the ships one day outrun our present limits, messages between stars will take years and messages between galaxies, by any means now known, will take millions. A network at that scale cannot be one mind thinking in one place. It has to be many local intelligences, each competent on its own, sharing what they have verified slowly and keeping receipts so that a fact can be trusted when it finally arrives. The light delay is the reason the mycelial design is the only one that works. A central brain would spend its whole existence waiting for its own thoughts to come back.

What lasts ten thousand years is a pattern. Objects decay; patterns can be rebuilt. The Ise Grand Shrine in Japan has been taken down and rebuilt on a twenty-year cycle since about the year 690, with one long interruption during the civil wars of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries; the sixty-second rebuilding was finished in 2013, and the building is never older than twenty years while the shrine is more than thirteen centuries old. The alphabet has outlived every empire that used it. The TCP/IP protocols that carry the internet have outlived most of the companies first built on them. Oracle7 is designed to be a pattern of that kind. Its core operations, bind, unbind and superpose, can be rebuilt by anyone from a written description in a few pages. No trained large model can be rebuilt from its paper; it exists only as its weights, and when the weights are lost it is gone. We intend to leave a protocol behind us, and the products built on it can come and go.

There is a limit on what the web should do when it arrives somewhere new. Our rule is to wake dead worlds and listen to living ones. Mars as far as anyone knows is barren, and seeding it is the kind of work this whole plan is for. Europa, the ice-covered moon of Jupiter, may hold an ocean with microbes in it, and if it does, seeding it would destroy a second origin of life before anyone had learned from it. Mycorrhizae join existing systems; they do not replace them. Wherever we find a living world, the network's first job is to listen, with the same discipline as the oak.

The same holds for the question of control. The settlement of other worlds can happen in two broad ways. It can be directed by whoever owns the ships and the intelligence that runs them, or it can be carried by many independent communities, each running its own systems and each connected to the others on equal terms. We have chosen the second without hedging. A decentralised intelligence is harder to build and harder to profit from. It is also the only kind we believe could persist through the political changes that ten thousand years will certainly bring, and the only kind that leaves the people at the far end of it free.

A hundred thousand years from now, nothing about this decade will be remembered except whether humanity used it to become a species that lives in more than one place. The most immediate condition for that outcome is mundane: whether the first open release of Oracle7 is received well enough that people start building on it. Everything rests on that, and we mean it practically. A web with no centre grows only if people choose to join it.

## IX. The fire

In Hesiod's telling, Prometheus steals fire from the gods, hides it in a fennel stalk and carries it down to people. In the tragedy attributed to Aeschylus he lists what came with it: numbers, writing, medicine, the reading of the seasons, the working of metals. Fire in that story is the first technology, and the gift is the capacity to make all the others.

The story does not end there, and we would be poor readers of it if we stopped at the gift. Zeus chains Prometheus to a rock and sends an eagle to eat his liver every day. And to humanity he sends Pandora, with the jar that releases every evil into the world. Fire is followed by its consequences. An intelligence placed in every car, house, street and forest is also, in the wrong hands, a surveillance system with no off switch, and we take that objection as the most serious one anyone can make against us.

Our answer is a set of design commitments, each of which can be checked. Oracle7 is released openly, in full, so that anyone can inspect it. It runs locally, on the device it serves, with no requirement to call home. Data stays where it is collected, and leaves only as verified facts that the place itself, or its people, have chosen to share. The way nodes talk to one another is an open protocol that anyone can implement. And there is no centre that anyone could seize, the founders of Prometheus7 included. The test we apply to every decision is whether the gift could be taken back. If any person or company, including us, could switch the network off or turn it against the people using it, we have built it wrong.

Prometheus means forethought. He is the one who looks ahead, and in some versions he knew his punishment in advance and gave the gift anyway. For an institute whose planning horizon is ten millennia, the name is accurate before it is anything else.

The seven carries the alchemists' sense of a transformation completed, but the meaning we hold to is older and closer to home. The Haudenosaunee teach that every decision should be weighed against its effect on the seventh generation, about two hundred years ahead. Oren Lyons, Faithkeeper of the Onondaga Nation, carried that principle to the United Nations and to the world. We take the same discipline and stretch it from seven generations to four hundred. Prometheus7 is the return of the transforming fire, and the fire this time is an intelligence that anyone can run, that tells the truth about what it knows, and that can be woven into a car, a house, a laboratory or a forest by the people who live with them.

It is a very large idea carried, for now, by a very small program. That program finds crash loops, reads certificates, scores its own forecasts and asks permission before it touches anything. It is an unlikely seed for a network spanning worlds. But the fungi began as threads too thin to see, and they have been at it for a billion years or more. We intend to work the same way: slowly, through many small connections, and without stopping. Prometheus7 succeeds when nobody needs it anymore.

## Coda. The land where the partridge drums

On the St. Lawrence River, where New York, Ontario and Quebec meet, there is a Mohawk community called Akwesasne. The name is usually given in English as the land where the partridge drums. It comes from the Kanien'kéha word for partridge, and the community describes the bird's drumming, once common along the riverbanks, as the heartbeat of the land. Another account says the drumming is the sound of river ice striking the rapids. Either way, the place is named for a sound the land makes. Most place names record a founder, a battle or a landmark. This one records an act of listening.

Our founder, psiloceyeben, is part Mohawk, and his family's line runs back to Akwesasne. He did not know any of what follows when he began designing Oracle7. The Akwesasne historian Darren Bonaparte describes the community as the capital of the seven communities that make up the Mohawk Nation: seven around one heartbeat. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy, of which the Mohawk are the Keepers of the Eastern Door, held sovereign nations together for centuries under a single Great Law without folding any of them into a centre, the arrangement the eighth section reaches for across worlds; in 1988 the United States Congress formally acknowledged the Confederacy's influence on the American Constitution. Haudenosaunee gatherings open with the Thanksgiving Address, which greets the world before any human business begins. And in the 1980s, when the river at Akwesasne was carrying industrial contamination from the plants upstream, the Mohawk midwife Katsi Cook and the women of the Mothers' Milk Project began measuring what was in their own water, their fish and their bodies, because no one else would tell them.

A people who name their home for the land's heartbeat. A nation of many communities held together without a centre. A practice of greeting the world before asking anything of it. A community that put instruments in its own river and kept its own records. psiloceyeben designed Oracle7 from first principles and from his reading of the substrate, and only afterwards found that his ancestors had already worked out its shape. He does not call this a coincidence. "Coincidence doesn't exist," he says.

Whatever one makes of that, one inheritance is easy to see. The seventh-generation discipline we described in the last section was theirs long before it was ours. The fire we hope to return was being kept, all along, by the people who listened to the drumming at the centre of the seven.
